What the network actually receives: your drawing cropped, scaled to fit 20 px and centred in a 28×28 grid, the same recipe its training drawings went through.

Each layer of this network is a stack of feature maps. Every cell in a map is computed from a small 3×3 neighbourhood of the layer before it, so early layers see tiny pieces of your stroke and later layers see whole shapes. Use the tabs to walk through it.

A 3×3 window, slid everywhere

A convolution reuses one small set of weights at every position: multiply the 9 pixels under the window by the 9 weights, add them up, add a bias, and keep the result only if it is above zero (ReLU). One filter makes one feature map.

Filter

The feature map fills in as the window moves. Click the pixel grid above, or any cell in the 3D view, to put the window there.

In conv 2 the window is 3×3 and 8 deep: it covers the same 3×3 spot on all eight maps of the layer below, so each output cell adds up 72 products. Weight sharing is the point: the same 72 weights are used at all 196 positions.

From local to global

No single cell in layer 1 knows what shape you drew: it sees a 3×3 speck. Each conv adds a ring of one cell, and each pool doubles the step between cells, so the patch of image a cell depends on (its receptive field) grows from 3 px to 18 px, and the final global max looks everywhere at once.

How much each layer knows

A simple linear classifier was trained on each layer's channels, each reduced to its strongest response anywhere (so position is thrown away). Average brightness alone is right 31% of the time. Knowing which edges appear gets 61%; which corners and curves, 82%; which parts of shapes, 100%.

How picky the channels are

For each channel, the share of its 200 strongest test responses that came from its favourite shape, averaged over the layer. Edges appear in every shape, so layer 1 channels are generalists; layer 3 channels specialise.

Why it picked that answer

The last step throws position away. Each of layer 3's 16 maps is reduced to one number, its strongest response anywhere (global max-pooling). Each class score is a weighted vote over those 16 numbers, and softmax turns the five scores into probabilities.

The votes

Each vote is a channel's best match times its weight for this shape. Teal votes push the score up, violet votes push it down. Click a vote to see where in your drawing that channel found its best match: the box above, and the cone in 3D.

Because each vote comes from one 18×18 window, every bit of evidence can be pointed to. The same window could sit anywhere in the image and give the same vote: that is what makes the answer independent of position.

A small network, really trained

Architecture: 28×28 input → conv 3×3 (8) → max-pool 2 → conv 3×3 (12) → max-pool 2 → conv 3×3 (16) → global max → 5 scores. Every convolution uses zero padding and ReLU.

It was trained from scratch with plain NumPy on 11,000 synthetic hand-drawn shapes (wobbly strokes, random size, aspect, rotation up to ±18°, stroke width and gaps) and tested on 1,500 drawings it never saw. The weights in this page are the trained weights, and every number you see is computed live in your browser.

Two choices make it easier to read. Layer-1 filters were kept summing to zero, so they react to changes in ink (edges) rather than to ink itself. Global max-pooling right after layer 3 means each channel's vote comes from one window in the image, so every vote can be traced back to the pixels behind it.

Channel names were written by hand after looking at each channel's strongest responses; the patches, class shares, tuning curves, probe accuracies and maps are computed. The network only knows these five shapes, so anything else (a letter, a scribble) still gets forced into one of them. The task is also easy: drawings are centred and scaled first, so even a plain linear model on the raw pixels does well. The point here is to watch how a convolutional network builds its answer, not to show that it needed to.

Inside a CNN · back-propagation by analogy

Shower lab

Training a network is like tuning a shower with many knobs. The shower only tells you one thing: too hot or too cold. Back-propagation works out, in one sweep from the shower head backwards, how much each knob is to blame. Then every knob is turned a little, all at the same time.

← Back to the 3D explorerFeature atlas →

Drag a knob up or down to turn it; drag anywhere else to orbit, scroll to zoom. Water colour is temperature and pipe thickness is how much of the shower's water flows through it. Teal beads running backwards are the blame signal.

Try this

Ten experiments

  1. One knob first. Choose One knob, press Backward pass twice, then Step. With one knob the blame is simple: every unit you turn it moves the shower 40°.
  2. The worked example. The shower is 36 °C and you want 38 °C. Step through the backward pass: the valve's blame is T_A − T_B = 10; the blame reaching knob a is diluted by the valve (0.6 × 40 = 24), knob c gets 0.4 × 40 = 16. One step turns all three knobs together and lands at 37.9 °C.
  3. Blame shrinks through stages. Turn the valve so b is about 0.1, then run the backward pass. Knob a barely reaches the shower any more, so it gets almost no blame and hardly moves. Deep layers far from the output get small signals the same way.
  4. A shut valve passes no blame. With b = 0, none of mixer A's water reaches you, so ∂T/∂a = 0 and knob a never learns. That is what happens behind a ReLU that is switched off (output zero): no signal comes back through it.
  5. Step size. Press Large and then Auto-train: each step over-corrects and the temperature swings hot and cold. Small gets there, slowly. The note under the slider says which regime you are in. Notice that the same step size can be fine with two stages and too big with one knob: how big a step is safe depends on how sensitive the shower is. This is the learning rate.
  6. One knob, used twice. Tick the shared-knob box. Now a single knob sets both mixers, so its blame is the sum of the blame from each place it is used (b × 40 + (1 − b) × 40). A convolution filter's weight is used at every position in the image, and its gradient is summed over all of them the same way.
Going deep

A three-layer shower

Choose Deep · 3 layers. Three mixers (layer 1) feed two valves (layer 2), which feed one output valve (layer 3). Mixer 2's water is split between both valves, the way one neuron feeds several neurons in the next layer. Six knobs, and the blame now has to pass through three stages.

  1. Three stages of blame. Press Backward pass four times and watch the blame travel layer by layer: the output valve first, then the two layer-2 valves, then the three mixers, then every knob's nudge. A layer-1 knob's blame is a product along its path: 40 × b₁ × c for mixer 1.
  2. Blame adds where paths meet. Mixer 2 reaches the shower by two routes, so its blame is the sum of both: 40 × (c × (1 − b₁) + (1 − c) × b₂). In a network, a neuron that feeds many others collects gradient from all of them.
  3. Vanishing blame. With c and b₁ small, mixer 1's water barely reaches the shower. Its blame is a product of small fractions, so it is tiny and the knob hardly moves, however long you train. Stack enough stages and early layers in a deep network learn very slowly for the same reason: the vanishing-gradient problem.
  4. Many right answers. Press Shuffle knobs, then Auto-train, a few times. Each run reaches the wanted temperature with a different set of six knob settings. Deep networks are the same: there are many equally good sets of weights, and where you start decides which one training finds.

The one thing to remember. The backward pass goes from the back to the front, but it moves information (blame), not the knobs. Nobody fixes the last knob first and the first knob later: once every knob's blame is known, all of them are nudged together, and the whole cycle repeats.
